Case study · Evoke

How Evoke v2 fixes the reasons people quit the first version.

Evoke is a Polish AI companion for difficult emotions: conversations, short exercises and a marketplace of psychologists. It earns on subscriptions and booked sessions, and for many people it is the step before therapy. The client came to us with a live MVP; I led the next iteration, v2, now in development.

Goal

Lift 30-day retention from 3.1% to 5% within three months of launch; the category median is 3.3%. Retention feeds both revenue streams: people subscribe and book only after weeks of use. No daily-use target on purpose: people open Evoke when something happens.

Problem

Only 3.1% of MVP users still opened Evoke 30 days after install, and both revenue streams went with them: 0.8% upgraded, 0.4% booked a specialist. The MVP asked for an account before the first word, cut conversations at a message limit, graded people's emotions and hid specialists in a tab few opened.

Prototyping

I tested the flows in Figma with 8 MVP users on the baseline's five tasks, with a psychologist on call.

Design

v2 kept the MVP's core: conversation, exercises, a growing profile and specialists. I reworked the five places where the funnel lost people and moved every screen onto a new design system: one typeface, blue and pink ramps and Material UI's 8px grid. A desirability test with 30 people chose between three directions: the MVP read as "impersonal" and "sterile", the winner as "calm" and "trustworthy". A second designer helped under my direction.

Decision 01

Evoke follows up on what you told it

MVP scores like "Emotional expression: 80" read as a report card and gave no reason to return. v2 keeps only numbers that describe, not grade, and when you mention an interview or a talk with your boss, Evoke asks afterwards how it went. The question comes right in the notification, "How did the talk with your boss go?", and follow-ups for any topic can be turned off in one tap.

Decision 02

From a conversation to a specialist

Most users aren't ready for a therapist's office; the chat is where they find out. When a topic keeps coming back, Evoke suggests a specialist in the chat, with prices one tap away, and the user approves the summary the psychologist sees. In a crisis the card leads with a crisis line. All four psychologists signed off on the flow.

Decision 03

Activity library

For evenings when people don't want to talk. Exercises are sorted by how you feel, not by technique. Photos won for one recommended exercise but turned into noise in a list of 80, so the mixed version stayed. Recommendations are signed "Rekomendacje Evoke", so the app speaks in one voice.

Decision 04

Limits that never cut a conversation

The MVP counted "4 of 200 messages" above the chat and cut people off mid-thought. v2 plans count conversations, and a started one can always be finished; the upgrade comes after it ends. 3 of 8 testers couldn't tell the plans apart, so the comparison now leads with what changes for you.

Decision 05

Talk first, sign up later

The MVP asked for a login before the first word, and 38% left right there. In v2 one screen comes first: what Evoke is, that it isn't a crisis service, and consent to process what you share. Then the conversation starts, and saving it with Apple, Google or email comes after. In testing, a sign-up prompt after three messages felt like a paywall, so it moved to the end of the first conversation.

Handoff

Mockups, specs and a Material UI theme went to development with psychologist-reviewed scripts. v2 is being built now, and the site is collecting a waitlist.
