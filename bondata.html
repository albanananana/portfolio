Bondata

Redesign of an HR analytics web app

Team
  • Me (Lead UX/UI designer)
  • 1 UX/UI designer
  • 2 frontend developers
  • The client's product owner
  • The platform's founder
Skills
  • Design systems
  • Data visualisation
  • Interaction design
Platform
Web app
Timeline
Mar – Jul 2026
Status
Handed off

Context

An employee-engagement analytics platform for HR leads and managers in Finland. Bondata reports over 100,000 users and more than 3,000,000 survey responses in a year, and HR leads read all of it through ~40 views and 25 chart types.

Problem

The data was there; reading it was the hard part. Filters sat in a side rail and applied only after Refresh. Cards had no type hierarchy and grew as tall as their data, objectives came without the numbers behind them, and 40 views sat in one flat list.

Outcome

I redrew every view across ~25 chart types and rebuilt filtering, typography, navigation and the card layout under them.

Ownership

I led the design and owned the audit, the navigation and filter architecture, the type hierarchy and the colour system. A second designer worked on parts of the chart library. Two frontend developers built it. Product decisions sat with the client's team, and I presented and defended mine in a twice-weekly review.

[ decisions ]

01

Twenty filters, in the wrong place

Up to 20 filters sat in a right-hand rail and changed nothing until you found Refresh at the bottom and pressed it. I moved them into a row above the content they control and made each one apply the moment it is set, so filtering went from set → Refresh → read to set → read. The row hides on scroll down and collapses to one sticky button; extra filters fold into "More".

  1. 1

    Filters in a right-hand rail. People look for them along the top or on the left, so a third placement costs a scan of the page before the first click. Jakob's law.

  2. 2

    A filter does nothing until you find Refresh at the bottom of the rail, so the page keeps showing numbers that no longer match the controls above them.

02

A card with everything at one size

Text-heavy cards set headline, value, comparison and explanation at close to the same size and weight, so reading a card meant reading all of it. I gave every card one type hierarchy, where each level of information has its own size and weight and the reading order shows before the words do. Bigger headlines cost vertical space on dense cards, which is part of why cards later needed a maximum height.

03

An objective you couldn't act on

An objective is a task a manager sets off the back of a survey result, and the old card carried a title, a paragraph of description and a four-step chevron bar. It never said which question the objective came from, which group it was set for or how that group had answered, and the discussion of it lived on another page.

I rebuilt the card around the decision it supports: the question, the group, the index and the result behind it ("64% agree, 6 of 12 responded"), comments that expand in place next to Important and Reply, and a dot-and-line stepper carrying the time in the current state instead of a coloured status bar. A manager reads, judges and answers an objective on one page, two cards to a row, where the old layout gave one centred card.

04

Cards as tall as their data

A card was as tall as its content, so one data-heavy card could run several screens and a results page turned into a scroll past everything you were not reading. I capped card height and let the content scroll inside the card, with a full-screen view for charts that need the room, so nothing hides behind a "read more". Card heights became a system of small, medium and full width, which also settles how many fit a row.

05

The AI summary didn't look like one

Every page already carried a generated summary: three paragraphs, open by default, with one line at the end reading "AI-generated summary may contain errors". You learned what you had been reading after you read it. I put an "AI Summary" badge above the text, gave the card the client's brand gradient that no chart uses, and collapsed it to two lines behind "Read more", so the reader sees where the words come from before reading them.

  1. 1

    The one line saying a machine wrote this sits at the bottom, in the same grey as everything else. You learn what you have been reading once you have read it, on a page about your own team.

[ What else changed ]

  • A chart palette refreshed on the model the backend already used: positive, negative, neutral, no colour.
  • A new icon set.
  • Trend indicators moved after the last word of a headline; corner positions jumped around on multi-line titles.
  • A feedback control on every card, five versions.
  • Comments, objectives, the measurement list, the admin tables and the login flows redrawn.
  • A focus/breakdown toggle, reworked because people could not tell which mode was on.
  • Trend indicators carry an arrow as well as a colour, and direction and valence stay separate, because on a harassment metric a rising number is the worse outcome. WCAG AA throughout.

[ Results ]

Every view in the product redrawn across ~25 chart types, on a design system built inside the project: 44 component sets, 136 colour variables across light and dark, 35 text styles, and 6,200 component instances across the final screens. Applying a filter went from three steps to one. Forty views got sections, search and a collapsed rail. Objective cards carry the question, the group, the index and the result behind them, two to a row against one centred card before. AI summaries are labelled at the top and collapsed to two lines. WCAG AA as the floor.

The work is handed off and nothing is released, so there are no product numbers. The two I would measure: time to a filtered view, and support volume around "which colour means what".

[ What I'd do differently ]

I argued every decision in a twice-weekly review and never put one in front of a user. Six versions of the collapsed navigation and five of the feedback control went through internal debate alone, when an afternoon with five HR managers from the client's own customers would have settled both.

[ What's next ]

The new theme runs behind an environment flag alongside the old product, so the rollout goes view by view rather than as a cutover. The usability session on the filter row and the card hierarchy is the first thing to run against it, and the two numbers above follow once the redesign is in front of customers.
